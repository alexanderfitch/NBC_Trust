Whatever path you choose, there comes a moment when your clients need to hear about it. Done well, it can deepen their trust. Done hurriedly, by letter, it can undo decades of it.

The difference is almost entirely about planning.

## Introduce people long before you announce anything

The best handovers begin with introductions that don't feel like a handover at all. Bring a colleague into review meetings. Let them handle a service request or two. Mention their name. When clients already know and like someone, news of a transition feels like a natural next step rather than a shock.

## Decide the order

Not every client should hear at the same time, or in the same way. Before announcing anything, sort your clients into groups:

1. **Your closest, longest relationships.** They should hear from you, personally, first, ideally in person.
2. **Clients with complex situations.** They will have the most questions; give them time.
3. **Everyone else.** A personal call or meeting where possible, followed by a written note.

Agree on the timing with your team so nobody hears it secondhand.

## Say the important things plainly

Clients usually want to know four things, and it's kinder to answer them before they're asked:

- **Who** will be looking after them, by name.
- **What changes,** and what stays the same: the team, the approach, the way they reach you.
- **When,** including how long you'll still be involved.
- **Why** you chose this path, and why you believe it's right for them.

## Make it a conversation, not an announcement

Leave space for questions and for feelings. Some clients will be happy for you. Some will feel a sense of loss. Both are signs of a relationship that mattered. Thank them, and follow up personally a few weeks later.

## Plan the conversation now

Even if any handover is years away, it's worth sketching this plan now. Doing so often reveals which clients haven't yet met anyone else at your firm. Introducing them is a small step you can start this month, and it's valuable whatever you decide.
