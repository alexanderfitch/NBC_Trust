Every small firm has an operating manual. In most of them, it lives in the owner's head.

It's the knowledge of which custodian form to use for which request, how a particular client likes to be contacted, what to do when a transfer stalls, and the hundred small judgment calls you make without noticing. It works brilliantly, right up until you want a week off.

## Why this one document matters so much

A written manual does three things at once:

- **It gives you time back,** because your team stops needing to ask.
- **It makes the firm steadier,** because it no longer depends on any one person's memory.
- **It makes the practice transferable,** because a successor, partner or acquirer can see how it runs and keep it running.

That third point is easy to miss. When anyone evaluates a practice, they're really asking how much of it would still work without the founder. A written manual is the clearest possible answer.

## Don't write it all at once

The manual that never gets written is the one you sit down to write in a weekend. Instead, write one process a week, starting with whatever someone asked you about most recently.

A useful page has five parts:

1. **What this is and when it happens.** One or two sentences.
2. **Who does it.** A role, not a name, where you can.
3. **The steps.** Short, numbered, in plain language.
4. **Where things live.** Forms, logins, folders, templates.
5. **What can go wrong.** And what to do about it.

## Let your team write it

You're the worst person to write most of these pages. You skip steps you no longer notice. Instead, have the person who does the task draft it, then read it together and fill the gaps. It takes a third of the time, and the result is more accurate.

## Start with these

If you don't know where to begin, these processes cover most of the questions owners get asked:

- Onboarding a new client, from first meeting to funded account
- Preparing for and following up on a review meeting
- Handling common service requests: distributions, address changes, beneficiary updates
- Billing and fee calculation
- What happens if the owner is unexpectedly unavailable

That last one is worth doing first. Many advisors are already expected to have some form of business continuity plan, and writing it often reveals exactly which other pages are missing.

## Keep it alive

A manual is only useful if it's current. Put it somewhere everyone can edit, and agree on one rule: whenever a process changes, the person who changed it updates the page that week.
