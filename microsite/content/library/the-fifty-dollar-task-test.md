Here is an uncomfortable exercise. For one week, every time you start a task, ask: *if I paid someone $50 an hour to do this, would the client notice the difference?*

If the honest answer is no, it's a $50 task. Most owners find that a surprising share of their week is made of them.

## Why owners hold on

It's rarely ego. It's usually one of three perfectly reasonable things:

- **It's faster to do it myself.** True the first time. Not true the fortieth.
- **Nobody else knows how.** Also true, because it has never been written down.
- **I'm the one who catches the mistakes.** Which means the process depends on you catching them, and that's the real problem.

None of these is a reason to keep the task. They're a list of what has to happen before you can let it go.

## Run the test for one week

Keep a plain list next to your keyboard. Every time you do something, jot it down with a rough time and one of three marks:

1. **Only me.** Advice, judgment, a relationship only you hold.
2. **Me for now.** Someone else could do it with instructions or training.
3. **Nobody.** It doesn't need doing, or a system could do it.

At the end of the week, add up the second and third columns. That number is the size of the opportunity.

## Hand off the easiest, not the biggest

The instinct is to start with the task that takes the most time. Resist it. Start with the one that is easiest to describe. You want an early win, a team member who feels trusted, and a first written process.

A good first handoff usually has three features: it happens often, it follows the same steps most times, and a mistake is easy to spot and fix. Scheduling, packet assembly and routine paperwork are classic candidates.

## Write it down as you hand it over

Sit with the person taking it on and do the task together once, narrating each step. They write it down, not you. That is the fastest way to find the steps you do without noticing, and it produces instructions in their words, which they will actually use.

Then check in once a week for a month, and stop checking in.

## The quiet payoff

Every task you hand off this way does two things. It gives you the hour back, which you'll feel immediately. And it leaves behind a written process that doesn't depend on you, which is exactly what anyone who might one day look after your clients will want to see.
