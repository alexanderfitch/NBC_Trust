When independent advisors start thinking about what comes next, the options can seem to narrow to two: sell to whoever offers the most, or keep going until you can't. In practice there are more choices than that, and each one asks something different of you.

This is a plain comparison, written for owners at the very beginning of thinking about it. It's general information, not advice about your situation. The right answer depends on your goals, your clients and your firm, and is worth talking through with your own legal, tax and compliance advisors.

## 1. Sell to an acquirer or aggregator

**What it is.** A larger firm buys your practice, usually with payments spread over several years and often tied to how many clients stay.

**What it tends to suit.** Owners who want a defined endpoint and are comfortable with their clients moving to a larger organization.

**What to ask.** Who, by name, will look after my clients? What happens to my staff? How much of the price depends on retention, and what happens if clients leave because of changes I don't control?

## 2. Merge with a peer firm

**What it is.** You combine with a similar-sized practice, often with a plan for one owner to step back over time.

**What it tends to suit.** Owners who want to keep working for a while, but with shared responsibility and a built-in successor.

**What to ask.** Do our client service philosophies really match? Who decides when we disagree? What happens if the other owner's plans change?

## 3. Develop an internal successor

**What it is.** Someone already at your firm, or hired for the purpose, gradually takes over relationships and ownership.

**What it tends to suit.** Owners with time on their side, and a strong preference for continuity for clients and staff.

**What to ask.** Is my successor ready, or could they be in a few years? How will they finance the transition? What's the plan if it doesn't work out?

## 4. Wind the practice down

**What it is.** You stop taking new clients, help existing clients find new advisors, and close the firm.

**What it tends to suit.** Owners for whom simplicity matters more than any sale value.

**What to ask.** Where will each of my clients go, and will I be comfortable with that? How will I handle records and regulatory obligations after I stop?

## 5. Partner with a trust company or institution

**What it is.** An established institution, such as a trust company, takes on the continuity of your client relationships, often with you involved through a deliberate, gradual handover.

**What it tends to suit.** Owners who want their clients looked after by a long-lived organization, and want a say in who and how.

**What to ask.** Who will actually work with my clients? How involved will I be, and for how long? What happens to my name and my staff?

## Questions worth answering for yourself first

Before talking to anyone, it helps to be able to answer these, even roughly:

1. What do I want my working week to look like in three to five years?
2. What matters most to me: price, timing, my clients' experience, my name, my staff?
3. How involved do I want to stay, and for how long?
4. Who do I already trust to look after my clients?

There are no wrong answers, and they will probably change. But owners who have thought them through tend to find every later conversation easier.
